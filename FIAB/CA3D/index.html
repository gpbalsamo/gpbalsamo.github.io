About, data and limitations

Model. CA-BRIS: MET Norway's BRIS (anemoi, 2.5 km limited area inside a global N320 grid) moved to a California Lambert grid and fine-tuned on CONUS404 (2017–2025, then a rollout stage), with the CONUS404 terrain. Started from the IFS analysis at 00 UTC; the global part is driven by AIFS Single. 72 h, output every 6 h.

Hourly frames are interpolated from the 6-hourly data: temperature, dewpoint and snow level with a monotone cubic Hermite curve, wind and cloud linearly; precipitation is spread over each 6 h window so that the hourly amounts add up exactly to the 6 h total. Hour h shows the precipitation of the hour ending at h.

Rain or snow. The share of snow comes from the height of the model cell against the snow level. Snow level is the height of the 0 °C wet-bulb temperature, from the 2 m temperature and dewpoint at the model surface and the temperature and humidity on the pressure levels above it (1000–500 hPa). Amounts are water equivalent, not snow depth.

3D cloud is drawn in the browser from the model's relative humidity on its pressure levels (1000–500 hPa: no high cloud): cloud where the humidity passes a threshold that rises from 60 % at 300 hPa to 90 % at 1000 hPa (the slider scales it), each level at its standard-atmosphere height, the volume ray-marched over the exaggerated terrain. It is an illustration of where the air is near saturation, not the model's cloud cover (that is the readout and the meteogram). Isobars are the mean sea-level pressure, smoothed, faded over terrain above ~1500 m where sea-level pressure is an extrapolation. The text forecast is written in the browser by fixed rules from the point's hourly values, not by a forecaster.

Close-up terrain. When you zoom in, the relief around the point you look at switches to the Copernicus DEM GLO-90 (90 m): tiles of about 415 m vertex spacing within ~240 km of the camera and about 105 m within ~55 km (the finest only where the relief exceeds 100 m). The weather is still on the model cells, so snow painted above the snow level now follows the finer ridges. With it, a terrain height of 1–2× looks natural. Copernicus DEM GLO-90 © DLR e.V. 2010–2014 and © Airbus Defence and Space GmbH 2014–2018, provided under COPERNICUS by the European Union and ESA.

3D buildings (pilot: San Francisco) are OpenStreetMap footprints, extruded in the browser when the camera is within ~20 km and the close-up terrain is on. Heights come from the height or building:levels tags where mapped, otherwise a typical height for the building type (houses 6 m), so many are approximate. They stand at true height on the terrain, whatever its exaggeration. Building data © OpenStreetMap contributors, available under the Open Database Licence (openstreetmap.org/copyright).

Coastline. Along the whole coast the shore of the close-up view is drawn from the OpenStreetMap coastline (land polygons, ~40 m pixels), so ports and reclaimed land show as land. Coastline data © OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL).

Terrain is the IFS TCo7999 climate orography (about 1.3 km; the whole-domain view is coarser, the regions under “Explore” are at about 1.25 km), vertically exaggerated. The weather is on the model cells (2.5 km), so the relief and the snow line painted on it are finer than the weather. Snow is painted where the terrain is above the snow level of the hour. Wind trails show the 10 m wind over the surface, not three-dimensional trajectories.

CONUS404 (training data): USGS HyTEST and NCAR. Contains modified ECMWF data © ECMWF, licensed under CC BY 4.0 (ECMWF terms). AIFS Single: CC BY 4.0. BRIS weights: MET Norway. Times are California local time. Built with Forecast-in-a-Box and anemoi; rendering with three.js (MIT). The idea of a 3D terrain explorer follows Neil Gordon's BRIS NZ “weather in three dimensions” demonstration; this page is independent code.

Provided as is, without any guarantee; not a basis for decisions. For official forecasts and warnings consult the National Weather Service. Place coordinates are approximate.
